Quantum Minesweeper
Find the hidden errors. Save the quantum state.
Overview
Quantum computers are extremely sensitive to noise. Even a single error can corrupt the quantum
information stored in a register. Quantum error correction (QEC) addresses this problem by encod-
ing quantum information across multiple physical qubits and detecting errors without destroying
the information being protected.
In this challenge, your quantum register is a minefield. Errors have been secretly introduced into
an encoded quantum state, and your job is to find and correct them.
Using quantum circuits, syndrome measurements, and error-correction strategies, your team must
recover the original quantum information across progressively more difficult error configurations.
Y our objective:Successfully complete as many error-correction levels as possible, demonstrate
your solutions through simulation, and implement your work on IQM quantum hardware.
1. The Challenge
Consider a logical qubit in an arbitrary state
|ψ⟩=α|0⟩+β|1⟩,|α| 2 +|β| 2 = 1.
By encoding this state into multiple physical qubits, we can protect it against certain quantum
errors.
However, somewhere in the encoded register, an error may have occurred. You do not know its
location.
Your task is to construct quantum circuits that:
1. Encode and protect the logical quantum state.
2. Extract information about errors through appropriate quantum measurements.
3. Identify the error syndrome and determine the appropriate recovery operation.
4. Recover the logical quantum information without destroying it.
For each level, the possible error types are specified, but the actual error location is hidden.
A correct solution must work for all permitted error configurations, not just one example.
Teams are responsible for implementing their own circuits, developing appropriate tests, and demon-
strating that their solutions work.

2. Challenge Levels
Level 1: Bit-Flip Mines
Error type:Pauli-X(bit flip)
Physical data qubits:3
Goal:Correct an unknown single-qubit bit flip.
Begin with the standard three-qubit repetition code:
|ψL⟩=α|000⟩+β|111⟩.
One of the three physical qubits may experience a bit-flip error:
E1 ={I, X0, X1, X2}.
Design a circuit that extracts the appropriate error syndrome, determines which qubit was affected,
and recovers the original logical state.
Your circuit must succeed regardless of which qubit experiences the error.
Success condition:Correctly recover the logical information for every permitted bit-flip config-
uration, including the no-error case.
Level 2: Phase-Flip Mines
Error type:Pauli-Z(phase flip)
Physical data qubits:3
Goal:Correct an unknown single-qubit phase flip.
Bit-flip protection alone is not sufficient to protect quantum information against every type of error.
In this level, the possible errors are
E2 ={I, Z0, Z1, Z2}.
Adapt the three-qubit repetition code to detect and correct phase errors.
Hint:Consider how the Hadamard gate transforms the Pauli operators:
HXH=Z, HZH=X.
Success condition:Correctly recover the logical information for every permitted phase-flip con-
figuration.

Level 3: Double-Crossed Mines
Error type:Pauli-Y(bit and phase flip)
Physical data qubits:3
Goal:Correct an error containing both bit-flip and phase-flip components.
Recall that
Y=iXZ.
Therefore, a Pauli-Yerror introduces both a bit-flip and a phase-flip component on the same
physical qubit.
The possible errors are
E3 ={I, Y0, Y1, Y2}.
You are guaranteed that any nontrivial injected error is of typeY, but its location is unknown.
Design a syndrome-extraction and recovery procedure that corrects these errors while preserving
the encoded logical information.
Success condition:Correctly recover the logical information for every permitted Pauli-Yerror
configuration.
Note:This level does not include independentXandZerrors on different physical qubits.
Level 4: Mystery Mines
Error type:Unknown Pauli error
Suggested physical data qubits:4
Goal:Detect and identify the type of an unknown single-qubit Pauli error.
The minefield now contains one of three possible error types:
E∈ {X, Y, Z}.
Neither the error type nor its location is revealed.
Your task is to construct an error-detection circuit that distinguishes between:
•No error.
•A bit-flip (X) error.
•A phase-flip (Z) error.
•A combined (Y) error.
Unlike the previous levels, full error correction is not required. Instead, you must use appropriate
syndrome measurements to identify the error type.
A four-qubit error-detecting code provides one possible approach.

3. Testing and Verification
Teams are responsible for developing and testing their own error-correction circuits.
For Levels 1–3, the objective is to recover the original logical quantum information.
For Level 4, the objective is to identify the error type through syndrome information.
Your testing should demonstrate that your implementation works across the permitted error con-
figurations rather than relying on a single successful example.
Testing Different Error Configurations
A robust implementation should work regardless of which permitted error occurs.
Teams should test different error locations, including the no-error case, and demonstrate that their
syndrome-extraction and recovery procedures behave as expected.
You are encouraged to develop automated tests that systematically evaluate your implementation.
Preserving Quantum Information
A successful implementation must protect quantum information, not merely reproduce classical
bits.
For example, consider testing logical states such as
|0⟩,|1⟩,|+⟩,|−⟩,|+i⟩,| −i⟩.
These tests help distinguish genuine quantum-state recovery from methods that only preserve
computational-basis information.
Teams should explain how they verify that the original quantum information has been recovered.
Demonstrating Your Results
During the live demonstration, teams should be prepared to show:
•Their encoding and error-correction circuits.
•How errors are introduced for testing.
•How syndrome measurements reveal information about the error.

4. IQM Hardware Implementation
As part of this challenge, teams will implement at least one of their quantum circuits using IQM
quantum hardware.
Teams are encouraged to develop and verify their circuits through classical simulation before im-
plementing them on IQM.
The IQM implementation and corresponding experimental results must be included in the final
submission.
Teams should be prepared to explain their implementation, present the results they obtained, and
discuss any differences between simulation and experiment.
Perfect experimental performance is not required. Understanding and explaining the behavior of
the implemented circuit is an important part of the challenge.
5. Challenge Requirements
Your project must:
•Implement a quantum error-detection or error-correction circuit.
•Successfully demonstrate at least one challenge level.
•Include a method for extracting and interpreting error syndromes.
•Test the implementation across relevant error configurations.
•Provide simulation results demonstrating the behavior of your implementation.
•Implement at least one circuit on IQM quantum hardware.
•Explain the underlying quantum error-correction principles.
Teams are encouraged to attempt additional levels and explore different error-correction strategies.
You are not required to develop a completely new quantum error-correcting code.
Existing QEC concepts and software libraries may be used, but teams must understand and explain
their implementations.
AI tools are permitted.

6. What to Submit
Each team must submit:

7. Assessment
Projects will be evaluated through their submitted implementations, experimental results, and live
demonstrations.
Judging will emphasize the team’s understanding of quantum error correction, the correctness of
its implementation, and the quality of its experimentation and verification.
The following criteria will guide the evaluation.
Technical Understanding and Correctness
Judges will evaluate how effectively the team understands and explains its error-correction methods.
This includes:
•Understanding the encoding of logical quantum information into physical qubits.
•Correct implementation and interpretation of error syndromes.
•Understanding how recovery operations protect the logical quantum state.
•Justifying circuit design decisions using quantum error-correction principles.
•Explaining why the proposed methods work for the permitted error configurations.
Teams should be prepared to walk through their circuits and explain the purpose of their individual
components.
Experimentation and Verification
Judges will consider how thoroughly and thoughtfully teams test their implementations.
